<p><strong>Could you briefly introduce yourself?</strong> </p><p>My name is Anas Abdelhakmi, and I am currently a PhD student in operations research at the National University of Singapore (NUS). My academic journey began in Morocco, where I earned my engineering degree in Industrial Management at EMINES, the School of Industrial Management of Mohammed VI Polytechnic University (UM6P).</p><p><strong>Why did you choose to study in France?</strong> </p><p>In fact, I didn't study in France. I was fortunate to receive an excellent education in Morocco, at UM6P. This program allowed me to stay in my home country while receiving a very high-level education, on par with the best international standards. Later, my desire to pursue cutting-edge research in my field took me to Singapore, to join the prestigious PhD program at NUS.</p><p><strong>Where did you study, and how did you settle in?</strong> </p><p>Arriving in Singapore was a real challenge, especially since I arrived in the middle of the COVID period. Adapting to this new culture was difficult at first. Health restrictions limited social interaction, which heightened the feeling of isolation. However, once the restrictions were lifted, I was able to discover the richness of life in Singapore and integrate fully, turning this initial ordeal into a highly formative experience.</p><p><strong>Did you notice differences between the education system you knew and that of your host country?</strong> </p><p>The excellent training I received at EMINES prepared me remarkably well for the high standards at NUS. The system here in Singapore is extremely competitive and strongly focused on innovation and cutting-edge research. While my studies at UM6P gave me very solid theoretical and practical foundations, NUS constantly pushes you to go beyond your limits, to publish in top-tier journals and to have a tangible impact in your research field.</p><p><strong>Did you join any student associations, clubs or networks?</strong> </p><p>Yes, absolutely. Beyond the academic sphere, I got involved in local social life. In particular, I am one of the administrators of one of the largest expatriate groups, organizing beach volleyball meetups. This has allowed me to meet people from all walks of life, share my passion for sports and build a real circle of friends outside the university, which has been essential to my well-being and my integration in Singapore.</p><p><strong>What were the most difficult moments of your journey?</strong> </p><p>The biggest challenges were cultural and personal. Adjusting to the food was one thing, but the most striking was the culture shock. I come from a very warm and open Moroccan culture, and I arrived in a more reserved society where people tend to keep to themselves. Overcoming that feeling of loneliness and learning to build new relationships in this context were among the hardest aspects of my integration.</p><p><strong>What was the most important thing you learned from your experience?</strong> </p><p>This experience truly accelerated my growth, both personally and academically.</p><p>On a personal level, finding myself in Singapore without the Moroccan network I was used to forced me to rebuild my social bearings. I created my own environment, which tremendously strengthened my adaptability and resilience.</p><p>Academically, it was a total immersion in excellence. I sharpened my skills by learning to lead complex research projects independently and, above all, to collaborate with world-renowned researchers. This profoundly broadened my vision of research and gave me the courage to aim for the highest scientific contributions.</p><p><strong>Did you take part in any projects, internships or formative experiences?</strong> </p><p>Yes, my path is firmly rooted in collaboration between academia and industry. I have been fortunate to work on concrete projects and gain highly formative experience in Singapore. For example, I work closely with renowned partners such as Singapore Airlines, where I handle the modeling of recommendation systems. I also had the opportunity to take part in a quantitative research mentoring program with J.P. Morgan, a crucial experience for applying my skills in finance. These collaborations are essential for turning theoretical research into real-world impact.</p><p><strong>Was the scholarship a driving force in your journey?</strong> </p><p>Absolutely. The scholarship was essential support, especially in a country with a cost of living as high as Singapore's. It gave me the financial security to focus fully on my research, but above all it opened a door to the region. It allowed me to take vacations to explore Southeast Asia, travel to Thailand and Indonesia, and truly immerse myself in new cultures. That has been a crucial source of personal enrichment, in addition to being able to go home to see my family in Morocco every summer.</p><p><strong>If you had to sum up your experience in one sentence or one word, what would it be?</strong> </p><p>From UM6P to NUS: adapt, learn, pass on, make an impact.</p><p><strong>Where do you see yourself in five years?</strong> </p><p>In five years, I see myself as a professor at a world-class academic research institution. My ambition is to found and lead my own laboratory and to make a significant contribution to advancing knowledge in my field. Even more importantly, I want to inspire the next generation of researchers and pass on to them the tools they need to achieve international academic excellence in turn.</p>
