<p><em><strong>1. Could you briefly introduce yourself?</strong></em></p><p>I'm Khaoula Riad, and I currently work as a quantitative developer at CFM, a systematic hedge fund. Before that, I spent a few years at BNP Paribas and then Natixis. My journey began at ENSIMAG, where I specialized in quantitative finance, which laid the foundations of my career at the intersection of mathematics, programming, and capital markets.</p><p><em><strong>2. Why did you choose to study in France?</strong></em></p><p>After my preparatory classes and the Moroccan and then French competitive entrance exams, joining ENSIMAG was a natural choice given the quality and reputation of its program. It was also an opportunity for me to step out of my bubble, live abroad, and become more independent. This stage shaped me as much personally as professionally.</p><p><em><strong>3. Where did you study in France, and how did your arrival go?</strong></em></p><p>I arrived for the first time in Grenoble, where I was going to study. Everything was new: the city, the system, the paperwork… The first few days were particularly difficult, especially having to deal with a flood of administrative tasks and find housing in the middle of September, once I was already there.</p><p><strong>4. Did you notice any differences between the French and Moroccan education systems?</strong></p><p>The biggest difference I noticed was mainly the contrast between the tightly supervised pace of preparatory classes and that of engineering schools, where the atmosphere is more “chill.” You are given much more autonomy: it is up to you to get organized, keep up with your classes, manage your time, and find your balance. I was also struck by how much importance is given to sports in France. The facilities, the clubs, the place of sports in student life… everything is designed to encourage students to take part. That is something we still need to develop a lot in Morocco.</p><p><strong>5. Did you join any student associations, clubs, or networks?</strong></p><p>During my studies, I joined several associations, including Solidarire, an association that carries out solidarity initiatives both locally and internationally. It was an experience that left a deep mark on me, and even one of the best of my student years. I am naturally drawn to volunteering, and this involvement allowed me to feel useful, meet wonderful people, and give meaning to what I was doing outside my studies.</p><p><strong>6. What were the most difficult moments of your journey?</strong></p><p>I did not go through any extremely difficult moment in the dramatic sense of the word, but if there was one more stressful period, it was clearly the search for internships in my second and third years. It is a phase where you have to send out lots of applications, deal with rejections, sometimes silence, and keep believing in yourself. Especially since it is your first real encounter with the professional world</p><p><strong>7. What was the most significant thing you learned during your time in France?</strong></p><p>What France taught me above all was to become truly independent. When you arrive alone in a new country and have to handle everything yourself, you grow up fast. At some point, you also realize that success is not just about grades, but that the people you meet and the network you build matter more: “your network is your net worth,” as the saying goes. And discovering capital markets, a very demanding and still very male-dominated field, teaches you that you have to know how to position yourself, make yourself heard, and above all prove to yourself that you belong there as much as anyone else.</p><p><strong>8. Was the scholarship a lever in your journey?</strong></p><p>The scholarship helps enormously, because it lets you focus on what matters most: your studies. You don't have the constant pressure of having to work on the side to make ends meet or look for student jobs. It takes a real mental load off your shoulders and lets you give your all to your studies.</p><p><strong>9. If you had to sum up your experience in one sentence or one word, what would it be?</strong></p><p>A demanding journey, but one that gave me the keys to build what comes next with confidence and to take charge of my future.</p><p><strong>10. Have you experienced any kind of career change?</strong></p><p>There was no career change as such, but rather a gradual progression and a logical continuation of my path.</p><p><strong>11. Where do you see yourself in five years?</strong></p><p>In five years, I would like to be able to return to Morocco, be closer to my family, and put everything I have learned abroad to good use, whether in the same field or through an entrepreneurial venture.</p><p><em>Thank you for sharing your story!</em></p>
