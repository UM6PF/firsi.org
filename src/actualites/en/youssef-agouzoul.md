<p><strong>1. Could you briefly introduce yourself?</strong></p><p>My name is Youssef Agouzoul, and I was born and raised in Casablanca. I am an engineer by training, and today I work as Product Marketing Manager at Metroscope.</p><p>Metroscope is a SaaS software company that provides nuclear and gas power plants with a solution to ensure their reliability and performance, building on the latest advances in digital twins and artificial intelligence.</p><p><strong>2. Why did you choose to study in France?</strong></p><p>The French higher education system has the twofold advantage of being both excellent and accessible, particularly financially. It also stands out for the diversity of fields it offers: whatever area interests you, you can find an institution able to support you in acquiring the skills you need.</p><p>You can also draw on a dense network of companies and industrial firms to put that knowledge into practice in professional settings through internships and apprenticeships. Finally, the closeness between the French and Moroccan education systems makes the transition smoother and more manageable, both academically and personally.</p><p><strong>3. Where did you study in France, and how did your arrival go?</strong></p><p>I first completed my preparatory classes at Lycée Henri-Poincaré in Nancy. I then entered ENSEEIHT, where I took courses in the department of fluid mechanics and its applications, particularly water and energy management. I rounded off my studies at a specialized school, IFP School, whose aim is to bring students as close as possible to the real-world challenges faced by industry. There, I chose to specialize in powertrains.</p><p>When I arrived, I was lucky enough to get a place in the school's boarding facility. Living in a boarding facility offers a gentle, well-supervised transition into a country you do not yet know. It provides the right conditions to gradually take on responsibilities outside the academic setting.</p><p>Arriving in a new country without the immediate support of your loved ones, while having to deal with administrative procedures and demanding studies, is no easy task. Appropriate support that reduces some of that uncertainty becomes especially valuable.</p><p><strong>4. Did you notice any differences between the French and Moroccan education systems?</strong></p><p>The Moroccan system places strong emphasis on theoretical fundamentals. You can easily see that the level in the core sciences is very high, especially in mathematics and physics. On the other hand, it is generally less demanding when it comes to languages, communication skills, and practical application.</p><p>The French system, as I experienced it in preparatory classes and at engineering school, is more balanced. It gives significant weight to putting theory into practice through tutorials and lab work, in settings close to the professional world. It also pays close attention to communication skills, which shows in the weight given to so-called humanities subjects in overall grades.</p><p><strong>5. Did you join any student associations, clubs, or networks?</strong></p><p>Yes, I was active in several associations related to education, a field that is particularly close to my heart.</p><p>In particular, I was a member of Skoole, which aims to digitize school textbooks through a “low-tech” solution that is accessible even without an internet connection. I also took part in Educ’Ride, an association committed to improving education in Morocco. We carried out two major initiatives: supporting teachers in using digital tools during Covid, and then the Bina’a program, which helps pupils aged 6 to 12 imagine and carry out projects for their school, to show them that their ideas can become reality.</p><p>These experiences taught me a great deal and left me with precious memories. I strongly recommend getting involved in associations during your studies: it is a concrete way to explore the real world and an excellent way to build a social life.</p><p><strong>6. What were the most difficult moments of your journey?</strong></p><p>The most difficult moments of my journey were often linked to decisions about which path to take. You hesitate between several options with little information, and it is hard to picture your future when your deepest aspirations are still unclear.</p><p>At such times, the support of inspiring people who are willing to share their experience is invaluable. It also takes initiative: attending events, joining networks, reaching out to people directly… steps that often make all the difference.</p><p>For some internships, for example, I relied on outside advice. In my first year of master's studies, I was torn between an internship in Toulouse and one at BMW in Munich, which involved a number of administrative and financial constraints. An alumnus of my school encouraged me to choose the more demanding option.</p><p>In the end, I spent four months in Munich, an extremely rewarding experience. Looking back, I do not regret any of the difficulties I went through: they quickly faded, but the experience stayed with me.</p><p><strong>7. What was the most significant thing you learned during your time in France?</strong></p><p>I learned how to learn. On several occasions, I had to start almost from scratch to reach a satisfactory level of competence in an entirely new field.</p><p>Building on my foundations in comprehension and analysis, I developed a method for tackling an unfamiliar subject: identifying the relevant resources, structuring my learning, and progressing step by step. It also means accepting that you will set aside some skills you will no longer maintain, and going through a learning phase of varying length that calls for resilience and perseverance.</p><p>For me, learning is like a muscle: you have to keep it active, all the more so at a time when everything is changing fast and we regularly need to acquire new skills while letting go of others.</p><p>The prerequisite is to cultivate the conviction that you are capable of learning, and to deliberately put yourself in situations that prove it to you. Over time, it is our learning experiences that end up anchoring this certainty. Holding this conviction is liberating: you are less afraid of making mistakes or of feeling locked into one role, because you know from experience that you can adapt.</p><p><strong>8. Did you carry out any projects, internships, or formative experiences?</strong></p><p>Every project, every experience, and every internship played a part in my development. The key element, for me, was daring to try new things. I have always sought to broaden the range of my skills while deepening them in certain areas. This regularly led me to step out of my comfort zone: moving to another country, discovering a new industry, taking on more responsibility…</p><p>Each of these experiences left its mark. They allowed me to confirm my interests and identify my strengths, but also, and this is just as important, to understand what I did not want to do. You have to want to go off the beaten track, to step aside, and to be, at times, disoriented by the unfamiliar.</p><p><strong>9. Was the scholarship a lever in your journey?</strong></p><p>Yes, the scholarship was a real source of empowerment. It made it easier for me to support myself and become independent at 21.</p><p>This financial autonomy gave me the freedom to make more personal choices, ones I fully owned and that were aligned with my deepest aspirations, without placing the weight of those decisions on the people around me.</p><p><strong>10. If you had to sum up your experience in one sentence or one word, what would it be?</strong></p><p>The word that comes naturally is: learning.</p><p>It sums up my journey well. Out of curiosity and interest, I have sought to discover new fields and to deepen my understanding of those I already knew. That was the case during my studies, when I chose specialized engineering schools and then a specialization, and it is still true today in my professional life.</p><p>I am drawn to subjects that I enjoy and that stimulate me intellectually. For that, the subject has to interest me, and I have to devote enough time to it to grasp its subtleties.</p><p><strong>11. Have you experienced any kind of career change?</strong></p><p>Yes, several times.</p><p>As an engineer by training, I first took on the responsibility of structuring the “Customer Success” function at my company, a department we created to support a growing number of subscription customers. This role, halfway between consulting and customer relations, was completely new to me.</p><p>To build up my skills, I went looking for information where it could be found: training courses led by professionals, reference books, and exchanges with other practitioners, in person or within specialized communities.</p><p>Today, I work in product marketing, once again a relatively new field for me. I apply the same approach: targeted reading, regular exchanges, training, lessons learned from experience… all ingredients that help me become more effective in this role.</p><p>Despite these transitions, nothing is ever lost: each experience brings added value and gives my profile a distinctive character, which ultimately becomes a real asset.</p><p><strong>12. Where do you see yourself in five years?</strong></p><p>In five years, I still see myself engaged in activities consistent with my values and aspirations. It is hard for me today to name a specific job title, but I know I will seek to use my strengths and my time to contribute to projects that have a positive impact on society.</p><p>I firmly believe that we all share some responsibility for building a better world and that, through our daily commitment, whatever its scale, we can contribute to it.</p><p>I also want to do things I genuinely enjoy: in my view, that is the best way to put energy and commitment into them and to make that contribution last.</p>
