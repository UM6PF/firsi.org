<p><strong>Could you briefly introduce yourself?</strong></p><p>My name is Zineb Karrakchou, I'm 30, and my path has been anything but conventional. After preparatory classes, I joined Grenoble École de Management, where I specialized in finance. The work-study program gave me my first professional experiences: first at Louis Vuitton in management control, working with the CFO, then at Sanofi, where I tracked Europe's industrial performance from a financial perspective. I then moved into strategy and management consulting before taking a new step and following my passion for cooking and travel, which led me all the way to Maison Lenôtre, working alongside Michelin-starred chefs.</p><p><strong>Why did you choose France for your studies, and how did you settle in?</strong></p><p>Continuing my studies in France was a logical next step. I was looking for a demanding environment, which naturally led me to preparatory classes (IPESUP), then to Grenoble École de Management.</p><p>Arriving in France on my own wasn't easy, but the Moroccan community was a valuable source of support. Looking back, I would advise students not to stay only within their own community: opening up to other cultures broadens your horizons and reveals paths to success you might never have imagined.</p><p><strong>During your studies, were you involved in any student associations or networks?</strong></p><p>Yes, I joined SOS, the humanitarian association of Grenoble École de Management, which worked in partnership with a local organization in Benin and Togo. There, I led awareness campaigns on diseases, the importance of education and gender equality. I also mentored micro-entrepreneurs, supported local NGOs and helped build a library for the village. This mission, one month in Benin and two weeks in Togo, was a defining experience that strengthened my attachment to the African continent.</p><p><strong>What were the most difficult moments of your journey?</strong></p><p>Two periods left a particular mark on me:</p><ul><li>After preparatory classes, when I didn't get into the Paris schools I was aiming for: it was a feeling of failure that was hard to overcome.</li><li>Toward the end of my time in consulting, when I felt my work lacked meaning.</li></ul><p>Looking back, these moments turned out to be the most formative. They pushed me out of my comfort zone, to reinvent myself and to broaden my perspectives.</p><p><strong>Could you tell us more about your professional experience?</strong></p><p>After graduating, I chose consulting, an excellent springboard for developing a wide range of skills in management and project steering while exploring different sectors.</p><p>· At a mid-sized firm whose clients were finance departments, I worked on topics such as cash management optimization, payment innovations (cashback, split payments…) and debt management. But the assignments were becoming repetitive.</p><p>· Finally, at Capgemini Invent, I had the opportunity to take part in a variety of projects across different sectors. There, I built solid expertise in project management and organization, whatever the complexity of the programs.</p><p>At the same time, I have always nurtured my passions for cooking and travel. Three years ago, I launched my food and travel blog, Taste.Wanderlust, to share my culinary discoveries and personal experiences.</p><p><strong>How did your passions for cooking and travel become part of your career path?</strong></p><p>As I approached 30, I felt the need to bring meaning back to my professional life. My role as a consultant was no longer stimulating, and obtaining French citizenship gave me the administrative security I needed to take the leap. At that precise moment, I felt there were old dreams buried inside me that I had to fulfill: traveling alone in East Asia and following my passion for cooking. I like to sum up this turning point with the motto: “chance favors only the prepared mind.”</p><p>So I left consulting to go on my trip, then pursue my passion by earning a CAP Cuisine (a French vocational cooking qualification) through the CFA des Chefs program, backed by five major French hotel groups. This intensive one-year program enables a fast career change while meeting the needs of the sector.</p><p>My trip to East Asia (Cambodia, Vietnam, Bali, Singapore) allowed me to explore local cuisines and to practice yoga and surfing. Back in France, I joined the École des Arts Culinaires Lenôtre as an apprentice chef. I was lucky enough to cook at the Stade de France and to work with two- and three-Michelin-starred chefs, Meilleurs Ouvriers de France, and the world's best caterer of 2023.</p><p><strong>What was the most important thing you learned in France?</strong></p><p>Three lessons stand out to me:</p><ul><li>Exceptional access to training: for example, thanks to the CFA des Chefs, I was able to train free of charge at one of the greatest culinary schools in the world.</li><li>Daring to take the plunge is often the hardest step. Once the decision is made, interviews, training and opportunities follow naturally, as long as you commit fully.</li><li>The logic of management is universal: organizing a digital transformation program for a large corporation or a dinner for 40 people rests on the same foundations.</li></ul><p><strong>Where do you see yourself in 5 years?</strong></p><p>I can probably see myself in Morocco, while remaining open to other opportunities. My ambition is to contribute to the development of the agri-food, restaurant and hospitality sectors, by working with prestigious hotel brands (such as the Royal Mansour or La Mamounia in Morocco) or with international food groups such as Danone or Sodexo (the group to which Lenôtre in fact belongs).</p><p>I also maintain a strong connection with Africa. My original plan was to take part in developing sustainable food systems on the continent, and I would like to be able to contribute to that alongside committed stakeholders.</p>
